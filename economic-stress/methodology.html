How this tracker works

Global Economic Stress & Resilience

This tracks economic and financial strain — anchored on the U.S. economy but factoring in global spillovers like China, the Eurozone, and commodity prices — alongside how much cushion exists to absorb that strain: room to cut interest rates, room to spend, and how healthy banks and balance sheets are.

The two scores

Economic Stress Index (ESI)
0–100, higher = worse

How much strain is building across growth, jobs, inflation, and markets.

Financial Resilience Index (FRI)
0–100, higher = better

How much room there is to absorb or respond to that strain.

What ESI looks at

  • Financial market stress (weighted highest) — bond markets, credit spreads, volatility; often the fastest warning sign.
  • Growth momentum — GDP and business-activity trends.
  • Labor market deterioration — unemployment, layoffs, jobless claims.
  • Household financial strain — real wages, debt payments, delinquencies.
  • Inflation pressure — how far prices are running above target.
  • Corporate and banking stress — bankruptcies, bank losses, liquidity problems.
  • Fiscal stress — the deficit and government-debt trajectory.

What FRI looks at

  • Banking system soundness (weighted highest) — bank capital and stress-test results; the classic amplifier of a downturn.
  • Monetary policy space — room for the Fed to cut rates without reigniting inflation.
  • Fiscal policy space — room for government spending without a debt crisis.
  • External buffers — dollar strength and demand for U.S. debt.
  • Corporate balance sheet health — how leveraged companies are.
  • Household balance sheet health — savings, debt, and net worth.
  • Labor safety net capacity — unemployment insurance and automatic stabilizers.

The Stress-Resilience Gap

Gap = FRI − ESI. A positive gap means there's more cushion than pressure right now; a negative gap means the cushion is thinning.

Good to know

  • Economic data doesn't arrive every week — jobless claims are weekly, but CPI is monthly and GDP is quarterly. If nothing new came out on an indicator, the tracker says so instead of manufacturing a move.
  • A policy response (like a rate cut) doesn't automatically mean the underlying stress is resolved — the two are tracked separately.
  • Hard data (official releases, market prices) is weighted above forecasts, commentary, or a single analyst's take.
