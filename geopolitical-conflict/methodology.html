How this tracker works

Global Conflict & Coverage

This tracks active conflicts around the world by how severe they actually are, and separately, by how much attention global media gives them — so it's easy to see which crises are being quietly ignored, and which get outsized attention relative to their toll.

Why there's no single "world conflict score"

Averaging unrelated wars into one number requires picking an arbitrary weighting scheme, and the result mostly just tracks whichever conflict has the highest death toll — which buries the exact imbalance this tracker exists to surface. So instead of one score, each conflict is ranked on its own, and the dashboard shows counts (how many are escalating, de-escalating, or newly emerging) alongside the rankings.

Severity — built from real damage, not headlines

  • Casualties and trend — from conflict-tracking datasets and corroborated estimates, not a single side's claim.
  • Displacement and humanitarian impact — refugees, internally displaced people, aid access.
  • Escalation trajectory — new weapons, new countries getting involved, territory changing hands.
  • Damage — destroyed infrastructure, disrupted trade or energy flows.
  • De-escalation signals — ceasefires that are actually holding, verified troop pullbacks.

Coverage gap — severity vs. attention

Each conflict gets ranked twice: once by severity, once by how much global/English-language media covers it. The difference between the two ranks is the gap.

severity this week severity last week coverage score

Positive gap = underreported relative to the damage (severe, but quiet). Negative gap = getting more attention than its severity rank alone would suggest — usually because of strategic stakes or a great-power connection, not because it's over-covered in any absolute sense.

Good to know

  • The watchlist isn't fixed — conflicts are added when they cross a real threshold and retired after weeks of no activity (they can always come back).
  • Large protests or lawful unrest aren't counted as "conflict" on their own — there has to be sustained armed violence or a real risk of it.
  • A "negative gap" conflict (like a major power standoff) can still be a very serious situation — the gap measures relative attention, not importance.
  • Disputed casualty figures are reported as a range with the source named, not presented as settled fact.
